Behind the game: how VectoFly is built

By the VectoFly team · Updated 11 November 2026

VectoFly looks like a simple arcade game, but a lot of work goes on underneath to make it fair, light enough for a phone and safe for everyone. This page explains the main ideas in plain language, for players who are curious and for anyone who wants to build a similar browser game.

Where it comes from

VectoFly’s original concept and low-poly look come from The Aviator by Karim Maaloul, published on Codrops under the MIT licence. VectoFly extends that idea with many more planes, maps, hazards, four difficulties, touch controls and a global leaderboard.

3D graphics in a web page

The game is drawn with Three.js, a library that talks to the browser’s WebGL graphics system, and is animated with GSAP TweenMax. Everything uses simple “flat-shaded” shapes: planes, trees, boats, buildings, mountains and hazards are built from boxes, cones and spheres rather than from big image files. That has three benefits:

  • The game loads quickly, because there is almost nothing to download.
  • It runs on modest phones, because simple geometry is cheap to draw.
  • It has a consistent, friendly look across all 50 maps and 32 planes.

The sky changes colour through four stages as your level rises, and each map defines its own palette, ground type, scenery and far backdrop.

Adapting to every screen

People play on 5-inch phones and 32-inch monitors, in portrait and landscape. The game measures its own area and adjusts the camera, the field of view and the spawn distances, so that obstacles are large enough to see and your reaction time is about the same everywhere. Scoring does not depend on screen size.

The obstacle director

Random obstacles would sometimes be impossible. VectoFly uses a “director” that builds waves from patterns and then checks them:

  • It spawns scattered groups, full walls with a gap, combs and bobbing spikes.
  • It always leaves a reachable gate, and each gate is placed close to the last one.
  • It keeps a minimum time between waves, a little longer on touch screens.
  • It keeps spikes away from pills and fuel cans, so pickups are not placed inside danger.
  • It adapts: difficulty scales with how you are doing, and it punishes staying in one place by sending a homing spike.

A second director schedules map hazards. It picks from the threats of your current map, never shows two big threats at once, gives every one a visible warning, and shortens the pauses as your level goes up.

A leaderboard you can trust

Online leaderboards attract cheaters, so VectoFly takes several steps:

  • The game compares the distance it counted with the distance that the physics reported. A mismatch ends the run with “Cheat detected” and nothing is saved.
  • It also checks the speed of each step and the base speed against the maximum for the level.
  • Each run gets a signed run ticket and sends run statistics, so the server can check that a score is plausible.
  • Implausible scores are rejected, and cheating entries are removed.
  • Your pilot name is generated randomly, so nobody can post their own text or personal information on the board.

Privacy by design

There are no accounts. Your coins, planes, maps and settings live in your browser’s local storage and never leave your device. Only your random pilot name, score and a few run details go to the leaderboard. Hosting is by Vercel, the leaderboard database is Google Firebase, and Google Analytics counts visits (you can opt out). Details are in the privacy policy.

What is next

New maps, planes and hazards are added over time, and the updates page lists every change. Many features, such as sliding anywhere on the screen to steer and the free starter planes, came straight from player feedback. If you have an idea, write to contact@vectofly.com.
